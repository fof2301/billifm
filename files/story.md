## PART 1 — THE WOUND (1994)

Bhopal, monsoon of 1994. **Haribhai Saluja** runs a small transport business in Old Bhopal — trucks, godowns, a house with a neem tree in the courtyard. His wife **Shakuntala** sings at the gurudwara on Sundays. Two sons: **Randheer**, 14, who wants to join the army, and **Chhotu**, 9, who follows his father everywhere and mimics voices — the milkman, the schoolmaster, film villains. Everyone laughs. *"Iski awaaz mein jaadu hai,"* Haribhai says. There is magic in this boy's voice.

That year, an extortion racket is squeezing the transport lines out of Bhopal. Police need a witness. They lean on **Dinanath Joshi** — a respected schoolteacher, freedom fighter's son, a man whose word carries weight in court.

Dinanath testifies that he saw Haribhai collecting extortion money outside the Hamidia Road godown on the night of 14 August.

He didn't. He saw *a* man. The police needed it to be Haribhai — the real racketeer, **Zorawar Sethi**, had paid well and needed a body for the case. Dinanath told himself it was probably true anyway; the Salujas were "that kind of family." He signed. He went home. He slept fine.

Haribhai gets eleven years. He writes letters home in a careful schoolboy hand — *"Shakuntala, neem ko paani dena. Chhotu se kehna papa ki awaaz yaad rakhe."* Tell Chhotu to remember his father's voice. In 2003, he dies in Central Jail — "cardiac arrest," the report says; the other inmates say otherwise. Shakuntala stops singing, then stops speaking, and is left at an institution in Sehore by relatives who stop visiting. Randheer runs away at 16; a decade later a man of his description dies unnamed in a rail accident near Itarsi. 

Chhotu — the boy with the magic voice — is passed between relatives who don't want him. He learns three things growing up: that the world listens to confident voices and ignores true ones; that his father was innocent; and that a schoolteacher named Dinanath Joshi built his family's coffin with one sentence.

He spends thirty years becoming nobody. No photographs. No documents. No face anyone remembers.

Only a voice.

## PART 2 — THE PRESENT: SERIAL SYNOPSIS (EPISODES 1–7)

**MEERA JOSHI**, 28, granddaughter of the late, beloved schoolteacher Dinanath Joshi. She runs *"Aakhri Awaaz"* — Bhopal's most popular true-crime podcast, recorded from a converted storeroom in her family home. Sharp, dry-humored, allergic to sentimentality. Her grandfather, who raised her after her parents' accident, is her moral north star. His framed photo hangs above her mic. Her only family now: **IQBAL CHACHA**, 61, her late father's closest friend and a retired Bhopal Police inspector — gravel voice, endless chai, a policeman's memory and a poet's regrets.

**Ep 1 — "Pehli Call."** Meera covers a strange spate of "voice extortions" — small businessmen receiving calls from a man who knows impossible details about their lives. No demands at first. Just proof that he *knows*. Then, ruin. She names him on air, half-joking: *The Voice.* That night, her podcast voicemail receives one message. Her own intro, played back to her, slowed down. Then: **"Sundar awaaz hai. Dada ji par gayi ho."** *Beautiful voice. You get it from your grandfather.*

**Ep 2 — "Jo Sunta Hai."** Meera investigates the victims. Each ruined man, she discovers, has one thing in common she can't yet see. Iqbal warns her off — "yeh professional hai, beta" — but pulls old case files anyway. The Voice calls a victim *while Meera is sitting in his shop*, and describes the color of her dupatta.

**Ep 3 — "1994."** A pattern surfaces: every victim is connected to one old case — *State vs. Haribhai Saluja*, 1994. The investigating officer. The court clerk. The man who bought the Saluja godown at auction. Meera pulls the case file and finds a name on the witness list that stops her breathing: **D. Joshi, schoolteacher.** She tells no one. Not even Iqbal. Especially not the podcast.

**Ep 4 — "Neem."** Meera visits the old Saluja house. The neem tree is still there; the house is a ruin. An old neighbor remembers the family — the singing mother, the boy who did voices. *"Woh bachcha... poora mohalla hansta tha. Kisi ki bhi awaaz nikal leta tha."* That night The Voice calls Meera directly for the first time. He doesn't threaten. He asks her about her podcast equipment, warmly, like an uncle. Then: **"Recording sambhal ke rakhna, Meera. Awaaz hi toh bachti hai aakhir mein."** *In the end, the voice is all that survives.*

**Ep 5 — "Sehore."** Meera traces Shakuntala to the Sehore institution. The old woman hasn't spoken in years — but when Meera plays her a recording of the gurudwara shabads, Shakuntala's lips move along. A nurse mentions the woman has one regular visitor, "her son" — who always calls ahead, never shows his face to cameras, and pays in cash. Meera has been in the visitor log room for six minutes when her phone rings: **"Maa se milne aayi ho? ...Achha laga."** He knows. He always knows. She realizes: he isn't watching her. He's *listening* — her calls, her mics, maybe her podcast studio itself.

**Ep 6 — "Gawah."** *The witness.* Meera confronts the retired police officer from the 1994 case, now dying and ready to talk: the testimony was bought, the real racketeer was Zorawar Sethi, and Dinanath Joshi — *"woh master ji"* — was pressured, yes, but he signed knowing. Meera's world cracks. Her grandfather — the man whose honesty she built a podcast, a personality, a life on — condemned an innocent man. She records the confession. She goes home and stares at Dada ji's photo above the mic for a long time. The episode ends with her deleting the recording. Then, at 3 AM, un-deleting it.

**Ep 7 — "Kabootar."** Meera decides to do the unthinkable: tell the story straight, on the podcast — her grandfather's guilt included. She writes the episode. She tells Iqbal everything; he goes pale, and admits he was a young constable in '94, that he "carried files" for that case, that everyone knew and no one spoke. Before she can record, the studio goes dark. A voice from the doorway — the first time it isn't on a phone: **"Episode achha likha hai. Par ending main sunaunga."** *You've written it well. But I'll narrate the ending.* Struggle. Static. The feed cuts.

The episode ends on found audio: Meera, breathing hard, whispering into a phone he doesn't know she has —

*"Agar koi sun raha hai... main zinda hoon. Aur main record kar rahi hoon."*

**If anyone is listening... I'm alive. And I'm recording.**

### THE CALL — live agent scene (OpenAI Realtime; behavior spec, not fixed script)

**Opening line (fixed):**
**THE VOICE:** *(calm, close, a smile in it)* Tum Meera nahi ho. ...Dilchasp. Toh tum woh ho jisse woh raat bhar phusphusaati rehti hai.

**Agent objectives, in order:** (1) learn who the listener is / how they know Meera; (2) extract what Meera has told them — especially the word *Sehore* or *gawah*; (3) plant doubt about Dinanath: he wants the listener to *know what her grandfather was.*

**Canonical behaviors:**
- If the listener stays silent: *"Chup? ...Hm. Chuppi bhi ek jawab hai. Maine gyarah saal chuppi suni hai — pehchaan leta hoon."*
- If the listener is aggressive: he gets *quieter*, warmer. *"Gussa. Achha hai. Gussa sach ke sabse kareeb hota hai."*
- If the listener lies: *"...Jhooth ki bhi ek awaaz hoti hai, pata hai? Halki si kharash. Tumhari abhi aayi."*
- The slip he ALWAYS delivers before hanging up, whatever was said (this lights the Khandaan Board):
**"Meera se kehna... Dinanath ji ki tabiyat ke baare mein poochha maine. ...Oh. Maaf karna. Bhool gaya — woh toh guzar gaye. Sab guzar jaate hain. Bas... kuchh log poora parivaar lekar guzarte hain."**
*(Tell Meera I asked after Dinanath ji's health. ...Oh, forgive me. I forgot — he's gone. Everyone goes. Some just take a whole family with them.)*
- Exit line if stonewalled 3 turns: **"Wafadaari. Izzat karta hoon main iski. ...Woh nahi karegi."** *(Loyalty. I respect it. She won't.)* — click.
- Hard limits: never states his name/identity; never references the listener's real world; 60–90 seconds total; max 2 sentences per turn.

## PART 5 — WHERE IT GOES (Ep 9, the finale — pitch only)

Sunday. The Voice sits Meera before her own microphone, in her own storeroom studio, beneath Dinanath's photograph, and asks her to record the final episode of *Aakhri Awaaz* — the true story of 1994 — in her voice, as he has written it. Meera says she'll do it. Then she does something he never planned for: she tells the truth *further* than his script — her grandfather's guilt, yes, but also the name his script conveniently spared: **Zorawar Sethi**, the man who bought the testimony, still alive, still respectable. The Voice, who spent thirty years wanting the world to *hear*, must choose between his revenge and the whole truth. The listener's line is open the entire time. Iqbal is coming. Shakuntala, in Sehore, is listening to the podcast a nurse left playing — and for the first time in twenty years, she speaks: her younger son's name.

The last sound of the serial is Chhotu's real voice — not The Voice, the boy's — saying the first honest sentence of his adult life.

**Theme, one line:** *a story about who gets believed — told through a medium that finally listens back.*
